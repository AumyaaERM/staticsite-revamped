## A new regulatory era — and a structural challenge most organisations are not ready for

On **13 November 2025**, India's Ministry of Electronics and Information Technology (MeitY) formally notified the **Digital Personal Data Protection Rules, 2025**, operationalising the DPDP Act, 2023 — India's first comprehensive data protection law. The notification triggered a phased **12 to 18 month compliance window**, giving organisations structured but limited time to align their operations with a fundamentally new regulatory framework.

For many organisations, the initial instinct is to treat DPDP compliance as a legal exercise: update privacy notices, revise consent flows, appoint a Data Protection Officer (DPO). These steps are necessary — but they are not sufficient.

The deeper challenge, and the one that will determine whether compliance is genuine or superficial, is **Data Governance.**

> The DPDP framework represents a material shift toward structured and enforceable data governance, demanding early planning, cross-functional coordination, and sustained compliance readiness.
> — *Lexology, 2025*

Without a functioning data governance framework, every other compliance obligation — consent, breach response, data subject rights, retention — becomes structurally impossible to fulfil reliably.

## What the DPDP Act demands from a data governance perspective

The DPDP Act, 2023 and the DPDP Rules, 2025 together impose obligations that are, at their core, **data governance requirements** — even when framed in legal language. Understanding this is the first step to building a compliance programme that will hold up to regulatory scrutiny.

### 1. Data mapping and data inventory as a legal foundation

The DPDP Act requires every **Data Fiduciary** to process personal data only for a specific, lawful purpose communicated at the point of collection. **Significant Data Fiduciaries (SDFs)** — organisations designated by the government based on data volume and sensitivity — must further demonstrate this through annual **Data Protection Impact Assessments (DPIAs)** and independent audits, with findings reported to the **Data Protection Board of India.**

None of this is possible without a comprehensive data inventory.

> The first critical step in DPDP compliance is conducting a comprehensive **data inventory and mapping exercise** — identifying every system, vendor, API, and workflow that collects, stores, or processes India-linked digital personal data.
> — *Scrut.io, 2025*

Without this map, organisations cannot define the purpose of processing, cannot enforce data minimisation, and cannot respond accurately when a regulator asks where a specific individual's data resides.

### 2. Consent management requires data lineage — not just a consent banner

The DPDP Act mandates that consent be **free, specific, informed, unconditional, unambiguous**, and given through a clear affirmative action before data processing begins. Crucially, individuals also have the right to **withdraw consent** — and when they do, processing must cease and data must be handled accordingly.

This makes consent management a **data infrastructure problem**, not a UX problem. To honour a withdrawal of consent reliably, an organisation must know precisely:

- Which data was collected under that consent
- Where it flows across systems
- Which downstream processes depend on it

That knowledge requires **data lineage** — a continuous, documented record of how data moves through the organisation from collection to deletion.

> Fragmented databases and inconsistent data practices will make compliance slow, risky, and costly. The need for unified and clean data management systems will become urgent.
> — *EY India, 2025*

Organisations running siloed data architectures will find consent management not just difficult — but **legally untenable** under the DPDP framework.

### 3. Breach notification within prescribed timelines demands real-time data visibility

The DPDP Rules, 2025 impose mandatory breach notification obligations: Data Fiduciaries must notify both affected Data Principals and the Data Protection Board **promptly** when a personal data breach occurs.

**Penalty exposure:**

> - Failure to notify the Data Protection Board and affected Data Principals following a breach: penalties of up to **₹200 crore**
> - Failure to maintain reasonable security safeguards: up to **₹250 crore** — the highest penalty under the DPDP Act
> — *EY India / DPDP Act, 2023*

Meeting these notification timelines requires knowing — the moment a breach is detected — exactly what data was compromised, whose data it is, and what downstream exposure exists. This level of real-time data visibility is only achievable through a mature data governance framework with comprehensive asset registers, access logs, and automated alerting built in.

### 4. Data Principal rights require data access infrastructure

Under the DPDP Act, individuals — referred to as **Data Principals** — have the right to access their personal data, request corrections or updates, and ask for erasure in specific circumstances. Data Fiduciaries must respond to these requests through structured processes.

Fulfilling these rights at scale requires data access infrastructure that most organisations simply do not yet have:

- A single, searchable view of where an individual's data sits across every system
- The ability to retrieve it accurately
- The capacity to delete it in a **Privacy-by-Design** manner that does not compromise data integrity elsewhere

## The five data governance capabilities every DPDP-compliant organisation must build

| Capability | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Enterprise data inventory and mapping** | A live, maintained register of all personal data assets, processing activities, vendor relationships, and cross-border data flows |
| **Consent lifecycle management** | Systems that capture, store, track, and act on consent status across every data subject and every processing purpose |
| **Data lineage and retention governance** | End-to-end documentation of data movement, transformation, and deletion — including automated enforcement of storage limitation and data minimisation obligations |
| **Access control and security governance** | Role-based access controls, audit logs, encryption standards, and anomaly detection that meet the reasonable security safeguards standard |
| **Privacy-by-Design governance** | Embedding DPIA processes, Privacy-by-Design principles, and data minimisation into product and technology development cycles — not retrofitted after the fact |

## The compliance window is open — but it will not stay open

The DPDP Rules, 2025 introduce an **18-month phased compliance timeline.**

> Industry experts agree that 12–18 months will pass quickly, particularly for companies without mature privacy and cyber infrastructure.
> — *India Briefing, 2025*

Organisations designated as **Significant Data Fiduciaries** face a **2027 full compliance deadline** for more demanding obligations including annual DPIAs, independent audits, and algorithmic due diligence for technical systems.

The organisations best positioned when enforcement begins are those that treat DPDP compliance not as a legal project — but as a **data governance transformation.**

> Businesses that act early by mapping data, redesigning consent, and strengthening internal governance will be better positioned to navigate India's evolving digital economy and maintain customer trust.
> — *India Briefing, 2025*

Data governance is not a supporting activity in the DPDP compliance programme. It is the **foundation** upon which every other obligation is built — and without it, no amount of policy documentation will constitute genuine compliance.
