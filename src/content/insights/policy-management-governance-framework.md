## Your Policies Are Outdated. Your Board Doesn't Know. Your Regulator Soon Will.

Most organisations have policies. Few have governance. There is a difference — and regulators know exactly how to find it.

A policy drafted three years ago, approved by a board that barely discussed it, communicated once via email, and never reviewed since is not a compliance asset. **It is a liability dressed as one.**

> The question regulators ask isn't "Do you have a policy?" It's: *"Show us the board minutes where it was discussed. Show us who owns it. Show us when it was last reviewed. Show us proof your staff know it exists."*

### What Good Policy Governance Actually Looks Like

- Every policy has a named owner — a person, not a department
- Board approval is evidenced by deliberation, not just a resolution
- Review cycles are tracked and triggered by regulatory changes
- Staff training is documented with acknowledgement records
- A policy register is inspection-ready at all times

### Who This Applies To

- **RBI-regulated NBFCs and banks** must maintain board-approved policies on credit, liquidity, outsourcing, KYC/AML, and IT security — reviewed annually as a minimum.
- **IRDAI-regulated insurers** must now maintain board-approved ESG, climate risk, and policyholder protection policies under CG Regulations 2024.
- **SEBI-listed entities** must publicly disclose board-approved governance policies — related-party transactions, whistleblower, dividend distribution — reviewed by the audit committee annually.
- **Companies Act entities** must adopt, approve, and file CSR, remuneration, and risk management policies within statutory timelines.

## Six Policies Every Regulated Entity Needs — and Most Can't Evidence

These are not optional. These are the policies regulators open first during an inspection — and the ones most commonly found to be outdated, unapproved, or unenforced.

### 01 — Credit & Lending Policy
What risks will you take, and at what limits? Who has authority to approve what? What happens when a borrower breaches covenants? If your board can't answer these from the policy, it needs rewriting.

### 02 — KYC / AML Policy
Customer risk categorisation, onboarding standards, suspicious transaction triggers, and escalation protocols. RBI expects this to be reviewed every time the PMLA rules change — not every five years.

### 03 — IT Security & Cyber Risk Policy
Access controls, incident response timelines, vendor security requirements, and data classification standards. Both IRDAI and RBI inspect this. Most entities have a document; few have a governance trail.

### 04 — Outsourcing & Vendor Management Policy
Materiality thresholds, due diligence standards, contract requirements, and ongoing monitoring protocols. Under RBI's outsourcing directions and IRDAI's PPHI Regulations 2024, this is non-negotiable.

### 05 — Whistleblower / Vigil Mechanism Policy
Reporting channels, anonymity protections, non-retaliation commitments, and board oversight of complaints. SEBI mandates this for listed entities. Its absence in practice — even when it exists on paper — is an audit finding.

### 06 — Risk Management Policy
Risk identification methodology, appetite statement, escalation matrix, and review cycle. For NBFC-ML and UL entities, this feeds directly into ICAAP. For insurers, it connects to the RMC mandate under CG Regulations 2024.

### What Happens When These Fail

| Regulator | Finding | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **RBI** | Policy outdated or unapproved | Adverse inspection finding, enhanced scrutiny |
| **IRDAI** | CG Regulations 2024 non-compliance | Penalty under Section 102, Insurance Act |
| **SEBI** | Undisclosed or unapproved governance policy | Adjudication, public censure |
| **MCA** | Missing or lapsed statutory policies | Qualified MR-3, officer-in-default liability |

## Fix It in Five Steps. Not Five Years.

Most organisations overcomplicate policy governance. Here is what it actually takes — structured simply, implemented seriously.

### Step 1 — Map
**Know what you need before you write anything.**

List every policy required by your applicable regulators. Assign a named owner to each — a person, not a team. Set a review frequency for each based on regulatory requirement and business criticality. This is your policy universe. Without it, you are guessing.

### Step 2 — Draft
**Write for the person who has to follow it, not the regulator who will inspect it.**

Structure: purpose → scope → who it applies to → what is required → what happens on breach → who owns it → when it is next reviewed. Legal and compliance review before it goes to the board. Plain language where possible. A policy no one understands cannot be complied with.

### Step 3 — Approve
**Get real board approval, not just a signature.**

Present the policy with a summary of key obligations, regulatory basis, and material changes from the prior version. The board discusses. The minutes reflect that discussion. The resolution records the effective date and next review date. This is what a regulator reads. Make it defensible.

### Step 4 — Embed
**A policy communicated once via email is not embedded. It is forgotten.**

Define who needs to know. Communicate through the right channels. Train where the policy requires behaviour change. Collect acknowledgement records. Build policy obligations into operational checklists and system controls where relevant. Test that people actually know what the policy requires.

### Step 5 — Review
**Set the trigger. Don't wait for the inspection.**

Annual review as a minimum for all critical policies. Immediate review triggered by any relevant regulatory amendment, significant business change, or audit finding. Document what changed and why. Obtain fresh board approval for material amendments. Archive the prior version. Update the policy register.
