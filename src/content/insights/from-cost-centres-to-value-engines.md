## The original premise no longer holds

When the first multinational Global Capability Center was established in India in 1985 — Texas Instruments setting up a software design facility in Bengaluru — the value proposition was straightforward: access skilled engineering talent at a fraction of the cost of operating onshore. For three decades, that logic held. GCCs were efficient, controllable, and financially justified on a single metric: cost reduction.

That premise has been fundamentally disrupted. India now hosts over **1,800 active GCCs** employing nearly **2 million professionals**, with the sector projected to generate **USD 110 billion in revenue by 2030** (NASSCOM). The organizations achieving the highest returns from their GCC investments are no longer measuring success in cost savings. They are measuring it in **innovation velocity, product ownership, revenue contribution, and strategic competitive advantage.**

The transformation from cost centre to strategic value engine is not hypothetical. It is already happening — and the organizations driving it are reshaping what enterprise GCC strategy looks like.

---

## What the shift looks like in practice

The most compelling evidence for GCC transformation is not in analyst projections — it is in the operating decisions of the world's largest enterprises.

### JP Morgan: from back-office support to global fintech innovation

JP Morgan employs over **55,000 professionals** across its India GCCs, making it the largest GCC employer in the country. Its India operations are not an extension of its back office — they are a core component of its global fintech innovation strategy. The India GCC is actively enhancing the organization's global fraud detection programme, reimagining payment processes, and deploying an internal digital assistant used across the enterprise. The platform architecture developed in India is now embedded in JP Morgan's global technology backbone.

This is not staff augmentation. This is **intellectual ownership at enterprise scale.**

### Google Pay: a product born in a GCC, deployed globally

Perhaps the most cited example of GCC-led product innovation is Google Pay, which was conceptualized and developed in India before being rolled out globally. What began as a locally relevant digital payments solution became a global product line built entirely from within a GCC structure.

> Over **half of GCCs** are now developing unique capabilities or leading organizational transformation, compared to just **18% a decade ago**.
> — *Thoughtworks, 2025*

The GCC is no longer a recipient of product requirements from headquarters — it is the origin of them.

### Goldman Sachs: GCC as a fintech R&D engine

Goldman Sachs' Bengaluru GCC functions as a global centre for advanced data analytics, algorithmic modelling, and risk engineering. It directly supports the firm's global asset management operations, trading platforms, and cybersecurity functions. Multiple Goldman Sachs leaders now describe India as one of the firm's **primary global offices**, not a secondary hub.

> *"GCCs are now building core business skills in investment risk rearchitecture — capabilities that directly reshape how the enterprise operates."*
> — *Pravin Goel, Goldman Sachs India MD (McKinsey Talks Operations, 2024)*

### Novartis: from delivery centre to R&D innovation accelerator

Novartis Biome India, a digital innovation hub in Hyderabad, exemplifies how life sciences GCCs have moved up the value chain. The centre integrates internal teams with an external innovation ecosystem through structured residencies and mentorship programmes. Novartis' India GCC established an in-house **Data Science Academy** whose graduates now contribute to predictive models in oncology research worldwide. The centre has been recognized as a **"Leadership Incubator"** — a talent model that scales cross-functional leadership and domain expertise at GCC level, not just technical headcount.

### PepsiCo: solving real operational problems with AI from the GCC

PepsiCo's Hyderabad GCC led the development of a multilingual AI chatbot for supply chain vendors across South Asia, **cutting query turnaround times by over 60%.** This is a precise example of a GCC solving a commercially significant operational problem — not through process outsourcing, but through purpose-built AI innovation originating from a regional centre with deep domain and market context that headquarters could not easily replicate.

## The three strategic levers behind high-performing GCCs

What distinguishes the organizations above from those still operating execution-only GCC models? Three consistent factors emerge across high-performing GCC transformation strategies.

### 1. Mandate expansion: from task execution to outcome ownership

High-value GCCs are not given tasks — they are given problems.

> **87%** of GCCs now own end-to-end global processes, and **45%** actively participate in global decision-making.
> — *EY GCC Pulse Survey, 2025*

This shift requires explicit mandate transfer from headquarters: product ownership, P&L accountability, innovation budget control, and the authority to define how global capabilities are built and deployed. Organizations that retain tight operational control from the centre consistently underperform those that extend genuine decision-making authority to their GCC leadership.

### 2. AI and analytics as delivery infrastructure, not bolt-ons

> **83%** of GCCs are already engaging with GenAI, and **58%** are actively developing agentic AI capabilities.
> — *EY GCC Pulse Survey, 2025*

The differentiator is not whether a GCC has adopted AI — most have. The differentiator is whether AI and advanced analytics are **embedded as core delivery infrastructure**, or treated as discrete experiments. GCC-led AI programmes tied directly to enterprise P&L goals, customer outcomes, and product roadmaps generate measurable business value. Those built in isolated innovation labs rarely survive the pilot stage.

### 3. Talent architecture designed for innovation, not execution

The talent model required for a strategic GCC is categorically different from one built for delivery efficiency. McKinsey's **"10/30/50 approach"** identifies the ideal innovation-ready GCC as one where:

- **10%** of enterprise leaders come from the GCC
- **30%** of the total employee base resides there
- **50%** of new-age skills are sourced from the centre

This is not a talent supply chain — it is a **talent headquarters.** Organizations building toward this model are actively co-creating curricula with universities, establishing in-house capability academies, and creating roles — AI governance architects, agentic workflow designers, responsible AI leads — that did not exist five years ago.

## The cost of standing still

The organizations that have not made this shift are not standing still — they are falling behind. GCCs anchored in legacy execution models struggle to attract and retain the talent required for AI, cloud, and product engineering work. They fail to contribute meaningfully to core business growth. And they increasingly find themselves disqualified from strategic enterprise conversations where innovation capability is the entry criterion.

> **More than 78%** of new GCCs established in 2025 were built on digital-first mandates.
> — *BW Businessworld, 2025*

The sector has already moved. The question is not whether GCC transformation is necessary — it is how quickly an organization can build the governance, talent, and technology infrastructure to make it real.

The organizations that answer that question decisively will find that their GCC is not a cost line on a balance sheet. **It is their most durable source of competitive advantage.**
