## India's net-zero commitment is reshaping how industries compete

India's commitment to achieving net-zero emissions by 2070 is no longer just a national climate goal — it is rapidly reshaping how industries operate, disclose, and compete. Alongside its target to reduce GDP emission intensity by **45% by 2030**, the country is accelerating sectoral decarbonisation through renewable energy expansion, energy efficiency mandates, green hydrogen initiatives, and the upcoming **Carbon Credit Trading Scheme (CCTS)**.

Under India's emerging carbon compliance framework, four energy-intensive sectors — aluminium, cement, chlor-alkali, and pulp & paper — have been assigned two-year emission intensity reduction targets across **282 plants nationwide.** The reduction trajectory is structured progressively, with nearly **40% of the target** expected to be achieved during 2025–26 and the remaining **60% during 2026–27.** Similar targets are expected to be rolled out for iron and steel, fertilizer, petroleum refining, petrochemicals, and textiles industries in the near future.

In this context, Indian corporates will have to strengthen and re-align their decarbonisation strategies. Until now, most organisations have focused on **Scope 1 and Scope 2 emissions** through operational efficiency, cleaner energy procurement, and direct emissions reduction. But for highly emission-intensive sectors, the majority of emissions often lie outside operational boundaries — across suppliers, transportation networks, outsourced manufacturing, product usage, and downstream activities.

These indirect emissions, known as **Scope 3 emissions**, are now emerging as the biggest challenge in corporate decarbonisation.

---

## The scale of the problem is much larger than most companies realise

Globally, Scope 3 emissions often account for the largest share of corporate emissions — in many sectors **exceeding 70–90% of total emissions.** In industries like steel, automotive, FMCG, chemicals, retail, logistics, and technology, operational emissions represent only a fraction of the climate impact. India is beginning to see the same reality emerge through disclosures.

> Reported Scope 3 emissions among **NSE Top 200 companies** increased from nearly **566 million tonnes CO₂e** in FY 2022-23 to approximately **1.24 billion tonnes CO₂e** in FY 2025-26 — driven not only by higher emissions, but by better reporting maturity and expanded disclosure boundaries.
> — *ICRA ESG Study, 2026*

> The share of NSE Top 200 companies **reporting Scope 3 emissions** increased from roughly **38%** in FY 2022-23 to nearly **60%** by FY 2025-26.
> — *ICRA ESG Study, 2026*

Even earlier disclosure trends pointed in the same direction:

> **51%** of India's top 100 listed companies disclosed Scope 3 emissions in FY 23, despite the disclosure remaining voluntary under BRSR at the time.
> — *PwC India, 2024*

This shift reflects a broader transition: Scope 3 is no longer being treated as an optional "leadership indicator." **It is increasingly becoming central to climate credibility.**

---

## Why Scope 3 has suddenly become a business challenge

The trigger is not sustainability reporting alone. The real driver is **global trade and capital flows.**

Under **CBAM**, European importers must increasingly account for the embedded emissions associated with imported products. For Indian exporters in sectors like steel, aluminium, cement, fertilisers, and hydrogen, carbon data is becoming commercially critical — and this is where Scope 3 becomes unavoidable.

Several CBAM readiness assessments now indicate that **European buyers are increasingly demanding traceable emissions data and audit-ready documentation** from Indian suppliers. In practice, ESG teams are now directly influencing export competitiveness.

India's regulatory landscape is evolving rapidly in parallel. Although full Scope 3 disclosure is not yet universally mandatory, the direction is increasingly clear:

- Scope 3 disclosures are currently covered voluntarily under the **'leadership indicators'** within Section C of BRSR
- ESG disclosures for the **value chain** have been made applicable to the **top 250 listed entities** (by market capitalisation) on a voluntary basis from FY 2025-26
- **SEBI's circular dated March 28th, 2025** defines value chain as encompassing upstream and downstream partners individually comprising **2% or more** of purchases and sales by value — with disclosure covering at least **75%** of total purchases and sales

This fundamentally extends ESG reporting from an internal exercise into a **supply-chain governance exercise.** Companies are now responsible not only for their own emissions tracking, but for their entire ecosystem of partners.

Most Indian manufacturers do not operate in vertically integrated ecosystems. Their emissions profile depends heavily on raw material suppliers, transportation partners, third-party processors, contract manufacturers, distributors, and downstream product usage. Without reliable supplier-level emissions data, companies risk:

- Higher assumed emissions values
- Increased carbon costs
- Shipment delays
- Audit failures
- Loss of buyer confidence

---

## The real challenge: India's supply chains are still carbon-data blind

The biggest issue is not intent. **It is infrastructure.**

Most Indian companies still lack mature systems for collecting value-chain emissions data at scale. Operational emissions are comparatively manageable — the data sits internally in electricity bills, fuel consumption records, plant operations, refrigerant leakage logs, and captive power generation data.

Scope 3 emission calculation requires data from **hundreds, sometimes thousands of external entities.** For Indian businesses, this is especially difficult because supply chains are fragmented, SME-driven, geographically dispersed, and digitally inconsistent. Many vendors lack the capacity to maintain emissions inventories, establish ESG reporting mechanisms, or demonstrate carbon accounting capability.

As a result, most companies continue relying on **spend-based estimates and generic emission factors** instead of supplier-specific data. That approach may have worked when Scope 3 reporting was largely voluntary. It is unlikely to remain sufficient going forward.

---

## Why many net-zero commitments still lack credibility

A growing number of Indian companies have announced net-zero ambitions, renewable energy targets, and decarbonisation strategies. However, many of these commitments remain heavily focused on operational emissions. **Without Scope 3 integration, climate commitments risk becoming structurally incomplete.**

- Automotive companies cannot ignore supplier emissions
- FMCG firms cannot exclude packaging and distribution
- Technology companies cannot overlook data centre supply chains
- Infrastructure companies cannot separate embodied carbon from project emissions

This is why investors increasingly evaluate not just reduction targets, but **reduction boundaries.** Incomplete Scope 3 coverage creates questions around transition risk, credibility, and long-term climate preparedness.

---

## The competitive advantage will come from data, not just decarbonisation

Before organisations can reduce Scope 3 emissions, they first need the ability to **measure them credibly.** That involves:

- Supplier engagement systems
- Digital MRV infrastructure
- Audit-ready documentation
- Traceable logistics data
- Standardised methodologies
- Integrated ESG data platforms

This is where ESG is shifting from **sustainability communication into operational intelligence.** The companies investing early in carbon data infrastructure are likely to gain stronger investor confidence, better export readiness, improved procurement positioning, and lower transition risk.

---

## What companies need to do next: turning Scope 3 from a reporting problem into a business strategy

> Supply-chain emissions (Scope 3) are, on average, **26 times higher** than direct operational emissions (Scope 1 and 2). Yet only **15% of companies globally** have set Scope 3 targets.
> — *CDP and BCG Joint Report, 2024*

This gap highlights where Indian companies now need to focus.

**1. Supplier segmentation and engagement**

According to the GHG Protocol, companies should initially focus on suppliers contributing to roughly **80% of procurement-related emissions or spend** — rather than attempting full value-chain coverage immediately. In practice, this means concentrating first on high-emission suppliers in raw materials, logistics, packaging, contract manufacturing, and energy-intensive components.

**2. Embed carbon criteria into procurement**

Leading companies globally are integrating **carbon performance into supplier evaluation and sourcing decisions** — embedding emissions criteria into supplier onboarding, vendor scorecards, procurement contracts, and sourcing decisions. This is especially relevant for Indian exporters facing CBAM scrutiny, where carbon-intensive suppliers could directly increase export costs and compliance exposure.

**3. Invest in digital MRV and emissions infrastructure**

> Nearly **40% of organisations** identified supplier emissions data gathering as their biggest decarbonisation challenge — rising to **55% among large enterprises.**
> — *Global Survey, 2024*

Companies are increasingly investing in digital MRV systems, supplier emissions portals, automated ESG data platforms, and traceable logistics and procurement datasets.

**4. Adopt collaborative supply-chain models**

Scope 3 cannot be solved by one company alone. Several industries are now sharing supplier emissions data frameworks, renewable energy transition programmes, and common reporting methodologies to improve value-chain transparency at sector level.

---

## The path forward

For Indian businesses, the next phase of ESG maturity will depend not just on expanding disclosures — but on **strengthening the underlying systems that support them.** Building measurable, traceable, and decarbonisation-ready supply chains will enable companies to:

- Improve the credibility, accuracy, and transparency of ESG reporting
- Align more effectively with evolving regulatory and investor expectations
- Support India's transition from standalone operational decarbonisation to **economy-wide climate transition** by 2070

The companies that move first on Scope 3 data infrastructure will not just be better prepared for regulation. **They will be better positioned to compete.**
